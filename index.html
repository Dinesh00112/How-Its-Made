01 Optics

Light meets the lens

Light bouncing off you enters a lens no wider than a pea. Inside sit five to seven plastic elements, each moulded into a precise aspheric curve.

Together they bend every ray from one point on your face onto one point of the sensor, a few millimetres behind. Phone apertures are fixed, typically f/1.7 to f/2.2.

To focus, a voice coil motor (a tiny magnet and coil) slides the whole lens stack by a fraction of a millimetre until the picture is sharp.

02 Optics

Filtering and steadying

Silicon sees infrared light our eyes can't, which would turn skin and plants a strange pink. A thin IR-cut filter glass in front of the sensor blocks it.

Your hands shake a little all the time. At a 1/30 s shutter speed that is enough to smear the whole picture.

Optical image stabilisation reads a gyroscope thousands of times a second and uses magnets to push the lens the opposite way, cancelling the shake before light ever lands.

03 Sensor

Millions of light buckets

The image lands on a CMOS sensor: a grid of 12 to 50 million photodiodes, each about 1 micrometre wide, a hundredth of a human hair.

A tiny microlens on top of each one funnels light into it. Each photon that hits knocks an electron loose, and the electrons pile up in a well while the shutter is open.

Brighter spots fill their wells further. That is all a sensor measures: how much light, not what colour.

04 Sensor

The colour mosaic

To capture colour, each photosite gets a tiny filter that lets through only red, green or blue.

They are laid out in a Bayer pattern: every 2×2 square has one red, one blue and two greens, because our eyes are most sensitive to green.

So each pixel knows just one colour. Two thirds of the colour information is missing. This green checkerboard is what the sensor actually recorded of you.

05 Sensor

Charge becomes numbers

When exposure ends, the sensor is read row by row, top to bottom, in about 10 to 30 ms. This is the "rolling shutter" that bends fast-moving things.

Each well's charge becomes a voltage, amplified by the analog gain. That is what your camera's ISO setting changes.

An analog-to-digital converter turns each voltage into a 10-bit number from 0 to 1023. Below are real values from your frame right now. Note the black level: even pure black reads about 64.

06 Transfer

The data highway

The sensor is its own chip. Its numbers travel to the phone's main processor (the SoC) over MIPI CSI-2: one clock lane and up to four data lanes, each a pair of wires carrying about 2.5 Gbit/s.

Each row is wrapped in a packet: a header saying "this is RAW10, this many bytes", the pixel data with four 10-bit pixels packed into five bytes, and a checksum to catch errors.

Traffic also flows the other way. Over a slow two-wire I²C bus, the processor tells the sensor what exposure time, gain and frame rate to use next, every single frame.

A 12 MP sensor at 30 frames per second sends 3.6 billion bits every second down these lanes.

07 Image signal processor

Cleaning up the raw data

The SoC has a dedicated image signal processor (ISP), hardware that runs billions of operations per second on every frame. First it subtracts the black level, and the grey fog lifts.

Lens shading correction: the lens delivers less light to the corners, so the ISP brightens them back to even.

Defect correction: a few photosites are "hot" and always read bright. The ISP spots pixels far brighter than all their neighbours and replaces them.

Noise reduction smooths random grain by averaging neighbours, but avoids averaging across edges so detail stays sharp.

08 Image signal processor

Filling in the colours

Each pixel still knows only one colour. Demosaicing estimates the two missing ones from the neighbours around it.

The simple version averages the nearest pixels of each colour. Real ISPs follow edges so they don't smear colour across them or leave zippered fringes.

Now every pixel has red, green and blue. But the picture looks green and washed out. Raw sensor colour isn't what our eyes see yet.

09 Image signal processor

Making it look right

Auto white balance works out the colour of the light and scales red and blue so white looks white.

A colour correction matrix, tuned in a lab for this exact sensor, maps its colours to standard ones and restores saturation.

A tone curve adds contrast and fits the scene's huge brightness range onto a screen.

Sharpening boosts fine edges, making up for softness from the lens and demosaicing.

10 Computational photography

Many frames, one photo

In dim light each photosite catches few photons, and the random noise takes over once the image is brightened.

So phones keep a rolling buffer of frames, even before you press the shutter, and merge a burst of them. Averaging N frames cuts the noise by √N.

The same trick powers HDR and night mode: short and long exposures are aligned and blended. Move while it stacks and you'll see why alignment matters.

11 Encoding

Squeezing it into a file

The encoder splits the photo into brightness (Y) and two colour-difference channels (Cb, Cr).

Our eyes notice detail in brightness far more than in colour, so colour is stored at quarter resolution (4:2:0).

The image is cut into 8×8 blocks, and a discrete cosine transform turns each into frequencies: smooth areas collapse into a few numbers in the corner.

Quantisation throws away the fine frequencies you won't miss. Lower the quality and the blocks show. The file size here comes from your browser's real JPEG encoder.

The numbers are compressed, tagged with EXIF metadata (time, exposure, ISO) and written to flash storage. Phones today often use HEIF, which follows the same ideas with smarter maths.

12 Display

Back to light

Open the gallery and the JPEG is decoded back into pixels, the reverse of everything you just scrolled through.

The GPU scales it to fit the screen, and the display controller refreshes the panel 60 to 120 times a second.

Look closely and each screen pixel is three tiny red, green and blue lights. From a normal distance, they blend back into you.

Light, lens, photosites, colour filters, analog-to-digital conversion, a multi-gigabit link, an image processor, an encoder, flash storage and a display. Your phone runs all of this up to 60 times a second just for the viewfinder.

How this demo works: browsers never hand out true sensor data, because the camera feed has already been processed by your device. This page undoes that processing (it removes the gamma curve, re-applies a Bayer mosaic, lens shading, black level, hot pixels and photon noise) to rebuild a realistic raw frame, then runs its own ISP forward, all on your GPU with WebGL. Nothing leaves your device.
